Practical housebuilding lessons learned:
- Communicate clearly and often. Stay in conversations as much as possible.

### Fighting for permits
At first, the town played nice with us. They supported our bid to have animals, hunt, fish, and grow crops on our couty property. I applied for my building permits. Right as we were about to sign, the planning director dropped a bomb on us. He said that building on land that was within 100ft of the city forced annexation. Annexation means you become part of the town, pay town taxes, and must tie in to the town water and sewer supply line. Practically for us, this meant we couldn't have the livestock we wanted. 

On further thought though, this didn't seem right. By his interpretation, a 400 acre plot of farmland bordering city limits would be annexed by this interpretation if you built a structure a mile from city limits. My plot directly bordered the city limits, and my current permit application had our house 75 feet away from the city sewer line. It sort of worked out 

The town left my permit in a stalled state for reasons unknown to us yet. Due to the nature of  Our lawyers talked this out, and the town's representative said they were definitely not conditioning permits  Mohsin and I dug into the town ordinances and found what the planning director was referencing. It turns out you must annex only if your house is within 100ft of a city utility. After clearing this up, I re-applied for permits with the house farther away from the sewer line. After my permit was left sitting again for a month, I had to poke and prod the town. Finally, the planning director told me the issue. The town was withholding our permits until we signed over the roadway to them. This is an illegal practice known as conditioning permits. The town manager told the planning director to "hold them up until we have what we need." We felt extorted! We just wanted protections for agricultural uses, outbuildings, hunting, and fishing. We went back and forth about whether legal protections were possible, and eventually needed to lawyer up. All this left us with a distrust for the good old boys club in Angier's town government. 

I brought this issue to the I didn't expect securing permits to take almost a year. But we had our permits and were able to break ground.

Grading
This land was an overgrown swamp. It had incredibly fertile soil, but it sat pretty low compared to the neighborhood beside it. The water table was also pretty high, so there was plenty of boggy, wet soil. We needed to raise the grade by a few inches over around 1.5 acres. If you do the math, that's a lot of dirt! My site prep guy had a creative idea to said we could be a dump site for fill dirt. When prepping the site for large projects like bridges or roads, you need to move a lot of dirt. Where does that dirt go? Civil engineers try their best to make the fills (addition) and the cuts (removal) even out to minimize the amount of soil that has to be trucked in or out to reduce the cost of a project. Dump truck drivers' services are expensive, and they pay dump fees if they have to put their dirt into a landfill. Our site, however, could be a free dump! So while we were securing the building permits, we got over 300 dump truck loads of dirt over four months for free while we were fighting for permits. We were able to build up a pad for the house and significantly regrade so the land pitched water to the nearby creek. What used to be a swampy low lying spot was now a peak. We had a plateau to build on with the land sloping away, which is ideal. Drainage, grading, and vegetation are the keys to controlling water. I learned this lesson repeatedly as the thick mud would continue to prove a challenge throughout the build.

Framing with the Kost crew
I originally set out on this venture to frame the house myself. Call it naievete, ignorance, or pride, but it was a bad decision. My HVAC sub convinced me of this after telling me I'd be spending every day for the next year framing this house. He asked me to imagine my kids as they are now. Then he asked me to picture my kids a year from now knowing I missed that entire year of their life nailing wood together. That convinced me to find a crew. 
I struggled to find a framing crew that I trusted. I had no friends who framed. The Amish builders in my state were too far away. As I was going to accept a quote from a father and son team, a friend of mine said he's starting a construction company with his brother. This seemed like my God-given sign. The Kost brothers talked with me for months leading up to the build, coordinating materials, asking questions about the plans. Finally, I signed their contract. We met up for ramen, and John's wife held my daughter as I signed and handed over a deposit. The Kost crew was happy to let me join in the work for a few days. That experience helped me scratch my itch to frame myself. The skills to frame aren't too complicated if you've done much carpentry or furniture building at all, it still is a ton of work. There are tasks that are far more efficient if you have a team of people. John and I did a deal while sitting on the top plates.
The trusses for half the house were a foot short because the engineers misread the wall height in this part of the house. The Kost crew and I worked on a game plan to ask for the money back. 

### MEP (Mechanical Electrical and Plumbing) Rough Ins
The plumber I thought I had in my corner ghosted me when it came time for it. I had to scramble for another plumber. Most of the plumbers I had referrals from didn't do new construction, but after asking each plumber who might I found one who does new home rough-ins. They moved much quicker than anticipated, starting the day after I met with the owner. They were done in about 4 days. 

My electricians were surprisingly great! After going through four different electrical quotes, I settled on a medium priced one. They ended up being familiar with custom homes, and knew exactly how to handle uncertainty in island sizes, room layouts, or even office layouts.

My original HVAC guy was too busy to do my house himself and let me help, so he subbed it out to a guy he trusted fully. After meeting his sub, I knew I was in good hands. We walked the house and planned out the duct locations, as well as keeping an extra supply for the crawlspace. 

Inspection time! I was nervous about the inspection, but I had no reason to be. We only had a few minor issues the first inspection: missing a few braces on a gable, needing to air seal and fireblock, and needing to print off the truss specs. The inspector mentioned that he couldn't read the notes on the truss spec that mentioned the bracket loads because the notes were in too small of a font. 

### Crawlspace care
A lot of moisture rose up from the ground, especially since our crawlspace was sunk into the ground by 2'. Without any vents in place The plumber put plastic sheeting down over the water to give themselves a clean area to work. I thought I'd be nice and leave the sheeting for the other trades, but that ended up backfiring. The ground released too much humidity, causing mold along almost all of the floor joists. After having a mold remediation crew come through, my friend and I furiosly encapsulated the crawlspace. After a full three days of work, we finished. We saved around $18,000 in labor! I installed a crawlspace dehumidifier and powered it with close to 250' of extension cords. Concerned about the voltage drop burning out the motor, I splurged for 12AWG extension cords. I then capped off the crawlspace with a custom door. With a remote humidity monitor next to the front door, I was able to verify that we were controlling the humidity pretty well now. 

### Siding
I could only get a hold of one contractor to quote me for the siding, and after pricing out the materials, $7.5k in labor didn't seem reasonable. So I bought the materials, watched a bunch of Youtube videos, and experimented with some scraps until I was confident enough to start. Solo siding is pretty difficult. Managing long, droopy pieces by yourself to get them level is a challenge, having to go up and down a ladder every time you need something really slows things down, and one person cutting with one person installing makes things much much faster. I was blessed enough to have an occaisional helper to hand me pieces, support the other end, or do the cutting while I hung pieces. Working through multiple heat advisories, eventually, I got it done. 

### Ethernet
Running ethernet cable was a refreshing change of pace from the siding. Mostly on ground, in the crawlspace, or on a small, light ladder for ceiling runs. I wasn't the most familiar with Ethernet hardware, but from what I understood Cat6 was what I wanted. Cat 6a would allow me to reach up to 10gbps speeds. We don't really game, we aren't particular about our video quality, and our 300mbps wireless meets our needs already. I wanted to have multiple access points across the house and security cameras on the four corners of the house, so I made sure to get thicker gauge cable and jacks that were POE++ rated for these runs.
